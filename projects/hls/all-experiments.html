Human Latent Space

Yourui Shao

As social beings, we humans can often develop some understanding or mental representation of another person by observing them in conversation. Such observation and understanding then allows us to, dually, guess at their next thoughts and behaviors and infer about them higher characteristics such as openness, honesty, and sociability. Thus, we are motivated to ask if we can develop an autoencoder for individuals, the representations of which allow us to infer characteristics and predict behavior. Mirroring how humans could develop such abstract representations, we seek to do so by learning to predict an individual's responses in naturalistic conversation in a self-supervised manner. Then, we evaluate such learned representations through correlation between each individual's representation and their reported and inferred behaviorial and personality characteristics.

Preliminaries

Dataset

We use the CANDOR dataset,[6] which is a corpus of transcribed video call conversations between paired strangers. This was a suitable fit since we wish to both have a large number of samples per individual across distinct conversations for train-test splitting, and a large number of participants from which to train our feature encoders and decoders. We obtain eligible 753 participants (personas) with a minimum, median, and maximum of 20, 130, and 705 training samples via the following criteria:

  1. We first isolate pairs from our transcripts, and group them by the speaker for contains the whole conversation prior to , though during training this is typically truncated to the maximum number of preceding turns that fit under a 512 token limit. We use the NaturalTurn transciption method[7] (mistakenly, we previously used the Cliffhanger and Backbiter segmentation algorithms, which introduced an abundance of secondary utterances like "yeah, um, yeah, yeah" or interjections that we worried introduced additional noise during training).
  2. First, we designate a minimum of 1 conversation per person in the global test set. This allows us to see if learned representations for each individual can generalize to response prediction in unseen conversations. We attempt to create as much overlap between the designated held out conversations for each individual, to retain a sufficient size for the train and val sets.
  3. After the test set is designated, we exclude participants for which there are no remaining conversations (i.e., all conversations were in the test set). If an individual's test set contains less than 10% of the samples of their combined train and val set, we reallocate more of that individual's conversations into the train set until the 10% test set allocation is satisfied.
  4. We divide the remaining non-test pairs between training and validation by designating 8 samples for validation and the remaining for training. Any participant with less than 8 remaining training samples is excluded at this point.

For experiments where fine-tune our base model , train a projector , or otherwise optimize any other component besides itself, we also hold out 20% (151) of the participants to test whether such optimizations are effective for learning new not seen in their training.

The CANDOR dataset provides a swath of known information about each participant that we can use for our characteristic features . These include a large number of personality and social behavior ratings usable as ordinal features, including self-surveyed and conversation partner-perceived Big Five personality scores. We also use categorical demographic information, such as sex and education level. We use these features to evaluate our persona vectors . We also create a set of competing surface-level features for each individual () with the train-set bag of words, filtered to a subset by retaining words which appear for at least 40% of participants, normalizing frequencies as within-participant proportions, and selecting the top 512 with the greatest variance. We also include as a a five-number summary for a feature of response lengths in words.

We also alternatively use the ICSI dataset,[8] a set of transcriptions of meeting recordings with repeating speaker identities. This dataset is much smaller, with 38 eligible participants and a minimum, median, and maximum of 34, 465, and 7275 train samples. As such, we'll primarily use it to measure technique transfer across datasets. Characteristic features were constructed from the supplementary MRDA corpus,[9] which provided counts of the occurrences of a variety of events such as questions, self-corrections, and "grabbing the floor" attributed to each participant, which we normalize by participant word and response count; and from leadership assignments,[10] where we compute the proportion of meetings where each participant was considered the effective leader.

It is unclear whether this is just a local minimum that the optimization process is struggling to overcome, or the global minimum with a flawed token-level cross entropy (NLL) training objective and insufficient training samples. A final identified cause for collapse was transcription quality. For CANDOR, the included Cliffhanger transcriptions included filler statements and verbal repetitions in the main channel, so repetitions were inherent to the training data, while the Backbiter transcriptions tend to include between-statement interruptions, making responses incoherent in isolation. NaturalTurn transcription[7-1] decomposes the conversation into fuller turns with less isolated filler words and interruptions. Thus, we later use the NaturalTurn transcriptions to test various optimization objectives, and still detect significant mode collapse, with repeated nonterminating sequences or short grammatical but generic responses like Yeah. or Ok. in both all-sample and individual sample supervised fine-tuning experiments (covered later). Conclusions

While the NLL objective shows promise for learning —producing containing tuning for at least within one gradient step—recall, however, that the NLL objective had originally caused to collapse. Furthermore, recall the two objectives we set out to simultaneously accomplish:

  1. Develop a method for encoding with decodability for and/or which could be reconstructed by (i.e., have tuning for) characteristic information .
  2. Have the same be usable for personalizing conversation response prediction to that individual.

Thus, the next step is thus locating an optimization objective () that simultaneously avoids mode collapse, with a minimizable , and also yields comparable tuning for characteristic features for gradient step-based . We can use supervised fine-tuning to screen candidate objectives. Such an optimization objective could then theoretically yield, after full multi-epoch gradient descent with , learned token embedding(s) that dually provides and retains tuning for characteristic features.

Our suggestions thus far—in particular, the gradient-based and encoder experiments, suggest that optimizing for accurate converation simulation () and developing representations containing characteristic information aren't strictly competing objectives, providing hope that an idea optimization objective can be found. There remain however a few possibilities for failure:

  1. First, that no can prevent mode collapse of This is plausible, given that conversational responses are often out of the natural range for a base model even for various prompts . Furthermore, with few samples per individual, excessive over-optimization can result in learning "shortcuts" or memorizing samples that reduce without generating coherent responses. Current objectives such as NLL, even when optimizing over the for all participants, with repairs such as the inclusion of , yield unrobust responses and incoherence. However, we have yet to experiment with a diverse set of optimization objectives, having concentrated mainly on optimizing methods for constructing with tuning for characteristic features. Furthermore, previous literature optimizing LLMs for personalization[15][16][17][18][19]demonstrate plausibility on artificial datasets or narrower tasks.

  2. Even if we locate such an , doesn't yield as strong encoding or decoding for . This I find less plausible, as the chosen are or can be combined with the NLL or cosine similarity objectives as ensemble losses, and such should have at least a proportion of the observed encoding or decoding for with those . However, if attained, this would either indicate that our objective of simulation is incompatible with good representation learning, and we should then approach each separately; or, more likely, that our selected prioritizes surface-level optimizations, or that the datasets used inherently do not retain any capturable character information () after we select an object that denoises such surface level granularities. This lattermost possibility would concur with our previous inability to significantly decode character level features when pooling semantic embeddings of , and suggest that previous observed encoding and decoding for are just correlates of with surface level features , such as vocabulary (bag of words).

  3. If there exist an yielding both coherent responses and strong encoding and decoding for in , the usability of one gradient step may not generalize over all optimizer steps and epochs. Overoptimization for could be detrimental to containing useful characteristic information, though it seems likely this was not detected by failure mode #2. We must take note that batch size may be significant, as uses all of a participant for a single step and optimization usually uses smaller batches sizes such as 8 or 16.

However, if we find a that does produce coherent output and the optimized on contains tuning for characteristic information, this also reversely verifies that the simulated responses will reflect not an individual's surface-level word choice, but their character and the associated sentiment and behavior. Thus, we conclude by once again reinforcing, optimistically, that our two goals are complementary. We could optimize for response simulation, but cannot be sure whether we are learning deeper behaviorial patterns unless our representations contain decoding for characteristics. We can teach an encoder to produce with maximal tuning for characteristics, but such would likely require capturing the relationship between —one capturable only through response prediction, a variant of which is autoregression.
